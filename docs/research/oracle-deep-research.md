Evidence-Based Workout-Block Design for a Forty-Five-Year-Old Martial Artist With Mid-Portion Achilles Tendinopathy and Prior Shoulder Stabilization
Executive synthesis and evidence grading
This is best treated as a capacity-building program rather than a collection of corrective exercises. The most consequential decision is the Achilles strategy: current evidence no longer supports the idea that eccentric-only heel drops are uniquely necessary. The 2024 JOSPT/AOPT clinical practice guideline recommends progressive tendon-loading exercise, using loads as high as tolerated, as first-line treatment for mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy; complete rest is not recommended. Eccentric-only training, heavy-slow resistance, and broader progressive-loading programs can all work.
For this athlete, I would therefore use a Silbernagel/modern-CPG-style progressive loading framework, while preserving the straight-knee and bent-knee heel-raise variants his physiotherapist already prescribed. The reasons are practical: it accommodates bodyweight plus a 7 kg dumbbell, can be distributed around real life, progresses naturally from slow strength toward energy-storage work, and does not require the very high daily repetition count of the classic Alfredson protocol. Lower-volume, “do-as-tolerated” eccentric loading has also performed comparably to the original 180-repetition/day Alfredson prescription in an RCT.
The second major programming decision is to stop treating passive flexibility as the limiting quality. Being halfway to the splits while cold already suggests substantial available passive ROM. The higher-return target for kicking is active ROM: hip-flexor force in high flexion, adductor/abductor control, trunk-pelvis control, and the ability to produce and absorb force at long muscle lengths. Resistance training through large ROM can improve flexibility similarly to conventional stretching, while also developing strength.
The third decision is that one 30-minute session is worth doing, but two weekly strength exposures are materially better. The 2026 ACSM position stand, synthesizing 137 reviews involving more than 30,000 participants, found that resistance training improves strength, muscle size, power and physical function across a wide range of prescriptions, while strength gains tend to be greater with heavier loads, full ROM, two to three sets, and at least two sessions weekly. WHO likewise recommends muscle-strengthening work for all major muscle groups on at least two days per week.
One weekly session can still produce or preserve meaningful strength, particularly when combined with kung fu and frequent micro-loading; it simply should not be portrayed as optimal for maximal strength.
Because the dumbbells top out at about 7 kg each, unilateral exercise, leverage, full ROM, pauses and high effort are useful—but slow tempo is not magic. Low loads can build muscle when effort is sufficiently high, but heavier loads remain superior for maximizing one-repetition-maximum strength. Eventually, 14 kg total will become too little for maximal lower-body strength, high-force tendon adaptation, and power development no matter how slowly it is lifted.
I use the following evidence labels below:
Label	Meaning in this review
Strong	Direct guideline recommendation and/or several consistent RCTs/systematic reviews.
Moderate	Reasonably consistent clinical evidence, but important indirectness, heterogeneity or limited condition-specific trials.
Emerging	Small trials, laboratory/EMG studies, biomechanical work, or evidence extrapolated from a related population.
Expert-opinion-only	A sensible clinical/programming inference for which the exact prescription has not been tested.
The prescriptions below are a starting point to take to the treating physiotherapist, particularly for the Achilles and previously stabilized shoulder.
Evidence review by research question
Mid-portion Achilles tendinopathy — Strong for progressive loading; moderate for the exact dose and pain rules; expert-opinion-only for distributed micro-dosing equivalence. Chimenti et al.'s 2024 JOSPT clinical practice guideline gives tendon-loading exercise first-line status and advises loading “as high as tolerated”; exercise should be performed regularly rather than managing the condition with rest alone.
Alfredson et al. (1998, American Journal of Sports Medicine) popularized 3 × 15 straight-knee plus 3 × 15 bent-knee eccentric heel drops, twice daily, seven days per week for 12 weeks—the famous 180-repetition daily protocol.
Beyer et al. (2015, American Journal of Sports Medicine) found heavy-slow resistance and Alfredson-style eccentric work produced similarly good clinical improvement; the HSR arm used three sessions per week and progressed from roughly 15RM toward 6RM with slow concentric and eccentric phases.
Stevens et al. (2014, JOSPT) subsequently showed a substantially lower-volume “do-as-tolerated” eccentric program was not inferior to the full Alfredson volume over six weeks.
Silbernagel et al. (2007, American Journal of Sports Medicine) showed that continued tendon-loading sporting activity, when governed by a pain-monitoring model, did not compromise rehabilitation outcomes compared with initial active rest.
The commonly used Silbernagel pain-monitoring model permits symptoms up to approximately 5/10 during/after activity provided they do not progressively escalate and have returned toward the person's normal baseline by the following morning; the later Silbernagel & Crossley (2015, JOSPT) return-to-sport framework is more conservative before running/jumping, recommending minimal ADL symptoms, around ≤2/10, and recovery between heavy tendon-loading exposures.
For this athlete I would deliberately target 0–3/10 during ordinary heel-raise work, treat 5/10 as a ceiling rather than a target, and use next-morning response as the decisive check because he also has a forefoot/sesamoid history.
A practical monitoring system is to record two things mentally each morning: first-step Achilles pain on a 0–10 scale and approximate stiffness duration. If either is clearly and repeatedly worse after a dose, repeat or reduce the preceding load rather than progressing it. There is no validated universal number of minutes of morning stiffness that defines an overload, so pretending there is one would overstate the evidence. The trend relative to that athlete's own baseline is more useful.
There are no good trials showing that opportunistic sets on escalators, stairs and throughout the day are equivalent to a deliberately progressive rehabilitation session. Reviews of Achilles exercise dosage have not established an optimal frequency, and the successful trials generally used identifiable, progressive exercise prescriptions.
I would therefore keep the micro-dosing, because adherence is excellent, but turn three days each week into “counted dose days” with enough total straight- and bent-knee work to track progression. Random extra sets are bonus volume, not the primary progression metric.
Morning training deserves a graded warm-up, not fear of the tendon. Acute work in healthy Achilles tendons demonstrates that warm-up alters tendon blood flow and mechanical behavior, although the specific study population and warm-up modes do not directly replicate a 45-year-old with tendinopathy.
There is no evidence that morning loading itself is forbidden. In practice, spend roughly five minutes moving from ankle pumps and easy bilateral raises to marching and then the first loaded set; do not make the first movement of the morning a hard single-leg deficit heel raise.
Twenty-plus years after anterior shoulder stabilization — Moderate for rotator-cuff/scapular conditioning; emerging for high-level gymnastics exposures; expert-opinion-only for permanent position restrictions. Rehabilitation literature for traumatic anterior instability consistently emphasizes restoring rotator-cuff strength, scapular control, proprioception and progressively sport-specific load. Ma et al. (2017, Current Reviews in Musculoskeletal Medicine) and the ASSET consensus guideline by Gaunt et al. (2010, JOSPT) support progressive cuff/scapular strengthening after stabilization.
The Bern Consensus Statement by Schwank et al. (2022, JOSPT) emphasizes criterion- and load-based return rather than simple time-based restrictions, while explicitly noting the shortage of high-quality RTS evidence for shoulder injuries.
There is no good evidence that a stable, asymptomatic shoulder decades after successful stabilization must permanently avoid all abduction/external rotation. That position is relevant because anterior instability classically occurs there, but a blanket lifelong prohibition is different from avoiding an uncontrolled loaded end-range. For this athlete, there is little reason to make maximum loaded 90°-abduction/ER mobility a goal. Behind-the-neck pressing likewise supplies no unique adaptation necessary for his goals, so omitting it indefinitely is a sensible risk-reward decision rather than an evidence-based medical prohibition.
Continuing pain-free overhead presses, push-ups and static handstands is defensible. No trial specifically establishes handstand safety 20 years after an open Bankart-type capsulorrhaphy, so that conclusion is load-tolerance reasoning, not direct evidence. Modern shoulder-athlete consensus supports retaining tolerated sport-specific loading rather than avoiding all overhead work.
The key stop signs are recurrent apprehension, a sense of translation/instability, pain that persists after the session, or a deterioration over subsequent days.
Back-handsprings are a different proposition. Biomechanical research confirms that back-handspring-type gymnastics tasks transmit substantial upper-extremity impact and require coordinated wrist-elbow-shoulder stiffness and alignment; the literature is not specific enough to derive a safe force threshold for this individual.
More importantly, his own February 2026 exposure caused a shoulder flare lasting months. That is strong individual evidence that current capacity did not match the task. Because back-handsprings add little that is necessary for his martial-arts or health goals, I would not include casual back-handspring practice in the current block. It becomes an optional separately prepared skill only after a sustained period of shoulder loading and professional clearance.
Right upper-buttock/lateral-hip pain with sitting — Strong exercise evidence only if the diagnosis is gluteal tendinopathy; moderate-to-emerging for the undiagnosed presentation. The symptom location and sitting provocation can arise from several structures. Gluteal tendinopathy commonly causes greater-trochanter/lateral hip pain; proximal hamstring tendinopathy often gives lower-buttock/ischial pain aggravated by sitting; deep-gluteal/piriformis-related syndromes can cause buttock pain aggravated by prolonged sitting; lumbar referral and sacroiliac-region pain remain possibilities. Sitting-provoked buttock pain alone cannot distinguish them.
For confirmed gluteal tendinopathy, Mellor et al.'s 2018 LEAP trial in BMJ is important: education/load management plus exercise outperformed wait-and-see and produced better global improvement than corticosteroid injection at long-term follow-up.
A 2025 systematic review likewise supports exercise plus education, while showing that the literature remains less definitive about the ideal exercise recipe.
Gluteal-tendon work by Grimaldi et al. emphasizes limiting unnecessary compressive positions such as sustained hip adduction while progressively loading the abductors.
The safest common denominator while the diagnosis remains uncertain is therefore: break up long sitting; use bridges, controlled hip hinges, side-lying abduction if comfortable, bird dogs, split squats and gradual single-leg loading; avoid repeatedly forcing the hip into whatever combination reproduces the buttock symptoms. Aggressive long-duration piriformis stretching is not automatically indicated for sitting-related buttock pain, and aggressive hamstring stretching can be counterproductive if proximal hamstring compression is involved. That cross-differential prescription is clinical reasoning rather than a validated universal protocol.
New bowel/bladder disturbance, saddle sensory loss, progressive motor weakness, severe or rapidly worsening neurological symptoms, major trauma, unexplained systemic illness/fever, cancer-related concern or severe unremitting night/rest pain warrant prompt medical assessment rather than an exercise experiment. These are standard serious-pathology screening features in current low-back/buttock-pain guidance.
Persistent or worsening symptoms, substantial radiation/numbness, a limp, or failure to improve over several weeks are also reasons for a conventional physiotherapy/medical examination.
Hip-flexor strengthening through and above 90° — Emerging directly; moderate by extrapolation from resistance-training principles. Juan et al. (2024) systematically reviewed hip-flexor activation during rehabilitation and strength exercises and found greater iliopsoas activity when exercises require greater hip flexion.
EMG activation is not proof that a particular drill will improve high kicks, but it makes the key programming principle plausible: do not train hip flexion only from neutral to 60° and expect maximum-specific strength above 90°.
With no bands or ankle weights, the best progression is mechanical: supine active leg raise → seated knee lift → unsupported seated compression lift → long-sitting straight-leg lift → 2–5-second end-range holds → slow kick chamber and extension. At the top end, the load is the limb plus progressively poorer leverage, not the dumbbell. When a bodyweight drill becomes easy, longer holds, slower lowers, a straighter knee and greater hip-flexion angle are preferable to balancing a 7 kg dumbbell precariously on the thigh. Above-90° work should feel muscular, not reproduce the old sharp anterior-hip pain.
Active flexibility, loaded lengthened work, PAILs/RAILs and evening stretching — Strong for static stretching and full-ROM resistance; emerging for end-range isometrics; expert-opinion-only for branded PAILs/RAILs and slow-kick protocols. Afonso et al. (2021) found no meaningful difference between strength training and stretching for ROM improvements across the included RCTs; Alizadeh et al. (2023, Sports Medicine) similarly found external-load resistance training improves ROM, with effects comparable to stretching in available comparisons.
This supports using split squats, lateral lunges, hip hinges and active leg lifts as mobility work rather than separating “strength” and “flexibility” into completely different worlds.
Static stretching itself also works. Ingram et al.'s 2025 Sports Medicine dose-response meta-analysis found chronic static stretching produces substantial ROM gains, with diminishing returns as weekly duration rises; its modeling suggested the chronic benefit tends to plateau around approximately ten minutes of stretching per muscle group per week, not that ten minutes is a minimum effective dose.
Konrad et al. likewise found chronic stretching improves ROM.
End-range isometric contractions have a reasonable physiological rationale because isometric adaptations are joint-angle specific and training at longer muscle lengths can produce useful strength/hypertrophy adaptations.
However, “PAILs/RAILs” as a branded protocol has not earned the same evidence grade as ordinary static stretching, PNF, or conventional resistance training. Use the principle—brief controlled contractions near end range—without assuming the branded sequence is uniquely effective.
Likewise, I found no high-quality trials showing that slow-motion martial-arts kicks or ballet-style leg lifts specifically increase adult martial-arts kicking height better than other active-ROM training. They remain highly sensible specific-practice drills, because they train control in the exact positions wanted, but the evidence grade is expert/practice-based.
There is no compelling evidence that stretching in the evening is intrinsically superior for flexibility. Evidence that bedtime stretching specifically improves sleep is limited.
Evening is nevertheless an excellent placement here because it separates longer passive stretching from the morning strength session and fits the athlete's adherence pattern. Long static stretching immediately before maximal strength/power work is less attractive; chronic evidence also suggests separating extensive stretching from resistance work may avoid small interference effects.
“Knees-over-toes” knee and ankle work — Moderate for full-ROM knee strengthening; emerging/indirect for backward walking; weak for the branded claims. Deep knee flexion is not inherently damaging in healthy knees. Rojas-Jaramillo et al. (2024) reviewed deep-squat biomechanics and concluded that deep squatting can be appropriate for healthy knees when load, technique and individual tolerance are suitable.
This supports gradually trained deep split squats and squats, not the idea that everyone must push maximum anterior knee travel immediately.
Backward walking has some clinical evidence: Alghadir et al. (2019) reported improvements in pain/function in people with knee osteoarthritis.
That is not evidence that backward walking prevents knee injury in an asymptomatic martial artist, and it is not proof that a sled is necessary. It is a reasonable low-load quadriceps/warm-up option.
Petersen/Poliquin-style step exercises are best viewed as ordinary controlled step-down/step-up variants. There is no compelling evidence that a specific branded angle uniquely “bulletproofs” the knee or selectively develops the VMO in a clinically special way. Similarly, tibialis raises certainly load the dorsiflexors, but evidence that standalone tibialis raises prevent knee injury is weak. The useful pieces of the popular approach are therefore unglamorous: progressive quadriceps strength, tolerating knee flexion through progressively larger ROM, ankle capacity and gradual exposure—not proprietary exercise names.
For this athlete, Achilles and sesamoid history override ideology: a deep split squat is useful only while the front heel can remain comfortably planted and the big-toe/forefoot and Achilles tolerate the dorsiflexion demand.
Early hallux valgus and previous sesamoid irritation — Moderate for intrinsic-foot exercise; emerging for barefoot/minimalist strategies. Jaffri et al. (2023, Journal of Athletic Training) found intrinsic-foot muscle training improved foot strength, balance, posture-related measures and some patient-reported outcomes, although pain effects were not consistently superior to controls.
Short-foot and toe-spread exercises activate intrinsic musculature, including abductor hallucis.
Hallux-valgus exercise trials suggest possible improvements in muscle function and modest alignment measures, but the literature is heterogeneous and exercise should not be sold as a guaranteed way of reversing structural hallux valgus.
Barefoot/minimalist exposure can stimulate foot musculature, but current reviews remain heterogeneous; changing toward minimalist/barefoot locomotion can also redistribute load toward the forefoot and calf-Achilles complex.
Consequently, barefoot controlled strength on the padded dojo mat is reasonable while the sesamoid remains quiet; high-repetition forefoot impact is not. Avoid interpreting “barefoot is natural” as evidence that more barefoot loading is always better.
With sesamoid symptoms, the relevant modification is to reduce painful first-MTP dorsiflexion and local forefoot pressure.
That means calf raises initially on flat ground rather than a large step deficit, front heel down in split squats, no prolonged loaded tiptoe positions, and no bouncing until both Achilles and sesamoid criteria are satisfied.
Minimal-dose training, isometrics and 5–7 kg dumbbells — Strong that small doses work; strong that greater frequency/load is generally better for maximal strength. WHO's health recommendation is at least two muscle-strengthening days weekly, and the 2026 ACSM position stand found ≥2 sessions/week and heavier loads generally favor maximal strength development.
Currier et al.'s 2023 BJSM network meta-analysis nevertheless found essentially all resistance-training prescriptions superior to no resistance training.
This makes one carefully designed weekly session substantially better than waiting for a schedule that supports an “optimal” routine.
Low loads can generate hypertrophy when sets are sufficiently challenging, but higher loads produce larger maximal-strength gains.
For ordinary strength exercises—not painful rehabilitation work—the final portion of a 40-second interval should therefore reach approximately RPE 8–9/10, with perhaps 1–3 technically sound repetitions left. Momentary failure is unnecessary; the 2026 ACSM review found no consistent advantage to always training to failure.
Forty-second horse-stance holds are legitimate strength/endurance work, but their adaptations are predominantly specific to the joint angles being trained. Isometric training can increase strength, with useful effects at longer muscle lengths, but it does not fully replace dynamic strength through ROM.
Use horse stance because it is martial-specific and time-efficient—not because isometrics possess a special joint-healing property.
For a cold morning, the ordering should be dynamic preparation → highest-priority technically demanding strength/tendon work → secondary strength → active-ROM skill. The strongest exercises should not be buried after exhaustive mobility circuits. ACSM's 2026 synthesis also found strength development tends to benefit when strength work is placed early in the session.
Lateral-chain asymmetry — Moderate that asymmetry is not automatically pathological; expert-opinion-only for exact correction dosage. Reviews of inter-limb asymmetry show that asymmetry is task-specific, measurement-dependent and common in healthy athletes; universal cutoffs such as 10% or 15% are not reliable diagnostic boundaries.
Do not try to manufacture perfect symmetry. Measure the same simple tasks monthly—comfortable standing side-bend reach, active side-leg/kick height, and perhaps side-plank endurance—under similar warm-up conditions. Train both sides equally as the default. If one side is consistently restricted across repeated measures and the restriction is not pain-protective, adding one extra 20–40-second set to the limited side for six to eight weeks is a sensible experiment, but that exact asymmetrical dose is expert programming rather than trial-derived medicine.
Training block library
The library is deliberately modular. A 40 s work/20 s transition interval is convenient for most exercises, but true tendon loading and high-quality strength should not be forced into 20-second rests when quality deteriorates. For Achilles sets that become genuinely difficult, 45–60 seconds of work followed by 45–90 seconds rest is preferable. This reflects the progressive-load evidence more faithfully than making the timer sovereign.

The aim is temperature, tendon ramping, hips/trunk and scapular readiness—not prolonged passive stretching.

Sitting provocation is described in deep-gluteal and proximal-hamstring presentations, though the exact ideal break interval has not been established.

Habit-anchored Achilles loading
This is the highest-priority micro-dose. The 2024 CPG favors regular high-tolerance tendon loading, and Silbernagel's model combines frequent exercise with heavier loading several times weekly.
Use three counted Achilles dose days per week. On each counted day, accumulate:
Exercise	Starting prescription	Progression
Straight-knee calf raise	2–3 × 40–60 s, slow; roughly 2–3 s up / 3 s down	Bilateral → bilateral up/single-leg down → full single-leg → 7 kg contralateral DB → larger ROM on step only if sesamoid is completely calm.
Bent-knee calf raise	2–3 × 40–60 s; knee kept flexed throughout	Same progression. A standing bent-knee single-leg raise is more challenging than a lightly loaded seated version with this equipment.
Optional mid-range calf isometric	2–3 × 30–45 s on irritable days	Use as a regression, not as a permanent substitute for dynamic loading.
The sets can be accumulated around existing anchors—stairs, escalators, brushing teeth, waiting for coffee—but count them. For example, one straight- and one bent-knee set at three naturally occurring points equals three sets of each by day's end. There is no trial proving that this distribution gives the same tendon adaptation as clustered sets; it is an adherence-oriented compromise.
When the exercise becomes substantially heavier and true fatigue/strength development is the goal, clustering the work with longer rests becomes preferable.
Do not “eccentric” the escalator uncontrollably. Every repetition should have a clear top and bottom, stable forefoot, and deliberate tempo. The left side may initially receive a lower load or bilateral assistance if its next-day response is worse; progression is driven by symptoms and capacity, not by forcing symmetry immediately.
Weekly architecture
The most realistic guaranteed 30-minute session is five minutes warm-up + twenty-minute strength block + approximately five minutes of counted Achilles work. A third Achilles set for each knee position can be completed later through a habit anchor. On a second session, rotate the other strength block and use the final five minutes for active kicking/hip-flexor work.
Because heavy/high-energy Achilles loading can require two to three days of recovery, Silbernagel & Crossley's return-to-sport framework recommends spacing demanding tendon exposures rather than simply adding them every day.
Slow calf-strength work is less energetically demanding than jumping, but the same load-response logic is useful.

Once martial arts again contains substantial bouncing, skipping or explosive directional work, the class itself should be counted as an energy-storage Achilles session, and the hardest calf-strength session should generally sit at least about 48 hours away.
For a one-session-only week, rotate Strength A and B week to week rather than trying to cram both into one 30-minute morning. A single rushed circuit touching every goal usually provides less useful stimulus than one coherent strength block. The daily active-kick work can also be attached to an existing martial-arts habit: two or three controlled high knee lifts or slow kicks while already changing clothes or preparing for class are more realistic than inventing a new app-driven “mobility session.”
The evening stretching block can be used on three to five evenings regardless of strength day, provided it stays relaxed. There is no requirement to stretch every night for ROM gains.

Likewise, true recurrent shoulder dislocation/subluxation or persistent apprehension deserves reassessment rather than exercise progression.
Return-of-spring roadmap
Returning to bounce should be thought of as a continuum from muscle/tendon strength → faster force production → low-level energy storage → unilateral/directional energy storage → martial-arts-specific spring, rather than a binary “no jumping / jumping” decision. The Achilles return-to-sport literature supports criterion-based progression and pain monitoring but does not provide a single universally validated clearance battery; published criteria vary.
Consequently, the exact thresholds below combine published clinical criteria with conservative expert programming.

Tendon recovery can remain prolonged even after symptoms improve.
The most useful simple Achilles readiness dashboard is therefore not a single test but a combination: low ADL pain, stable morning symptoms, good single-leg heel-raise capacity, tolerance of the immediately previous energy-storage level, and no sesamoid reaction. That is much more defensible than waiting for complete absence of every tendon sensation before exposing it to spring.
Back-handspring work belongs on a separate shoulder-risk track, not merely at the end of the Achilles progression. Its entry criteria should be considerably stricter: no lingering shoulder flare or instability sensation; comfortable overhead ROM; treating-physio confidence in the repaired shoulder; repeated pain-free push-ups and overhead presses; pain/apprehension-free static handstands; then controlled handstand weight shifts and progressively faster coached upper-extremity loading before a full skill attempt. Modern shoulder consensus supports criterion-based progression, while gymnastics biomechanics confirm that the skill imposes impact through the upper extremity.
From a pure health-and-martial-arts risk-benefit perspective, however, I would not make returning to back-handsprings a program goal. Nothing in the evidence suggests that back-handsprings confer a joint-health or martial-arts benefit that cannot be obtained more safely from handstands, push-ups, overhead strength, controlled tumbling preparation and lower-limb spring work. Given the months-long flare after the February 2026 attempt, reintroduction is defensible only if the skill itself matters enough to justify dedicated gymnastics preparation and physiotherapist/coaching oversight. That final judgment is necessarily expert-opinion-only, because no study answers the highly specific question of back-handspring risk in a 45-year-old, two decades after open anterior capsular stabilization.
